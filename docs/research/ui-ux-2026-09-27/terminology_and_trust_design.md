# Terminology, metaphor and trust-building visual design for Flying Money (as of 2026-09-27)

Scope note: research notes for the report writer. Product context comes from `docs/copy/copy-deck.md` (v1, 24 Sep 2026), which already makes a few big calls: "budget" for agents and "certificate" for people, never both on one screen; drop "sealed"; "payment slip" instead of "note"; "Collect" instead of "redeem"; "Dashboard" as the nav label for "Counting House"; "digital dollars (USDC)" once per page; and technical terms only in /docs. Where a finding bears on one of those calls, the Inferences say so.

## 1. Metaphors and skeuomorphism for new financial ideas: when they help and when they mislead (including crypto terms)

### Takeaway
Metaphors and skeuomorphic cues help first-time learning only when they map onto a mental model users already have. Crypto research shows that familiar-sounding terms ("wallet", "sign", "approve", "seed phrase") often carry over a *wrong* model, and that is where security and money losses come from. The design rule from the SOUPS literature is to build tools that stay safe even when users' mental models are wrong, rather than to force users into a correct model.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g (15 Mar 2024) describes skeuomorphism as useful as "a functional learning aid" that grounds unfamiliar interfaces in real-world objects and existing mental models. It warns that heavy realism produced cluttered, slow, and sometimes "clunky and unintuitive" interactions. NN/g gives no explicit guidance on metaphors for concepts with no real-world counterpart. — [NN/g, Skeuomorphism](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeuomorphism/)
- Mixed evidence on learnability: an HCI 2018 study found that older and less experienced users had *more* trouble with "naturalistic" skeuomorphic interfaces than with abstract flat ones, which casts doubt on the idea that realism always helps novices. — [ScienceOpen, Affordances and Metaphors Revisited (BCS HCI 2018)](https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.14236/ewic/HCI2018.57); summarized in [CareerFoundry](https://careerfoundry.com/en/blog/ux-design/what-is-skeuomorphism/)
- NN/g criticizes fully flat design for removing signifiers (it argues for "Flat 2.0", which keeps subtle cues such as shadows on clickable elements). A secondary source says NN/g's 2017 study found flat interfaces ~22% slower. I did not verify that figure on NN/g's page. — [NN/g, Flat Design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-design/); figure via [search summary / CareerFoundry](https://careerfoundry.com/en/blog/ux-design/what-is-skeuomorphism/)
- SOUPS 2020 (Mai, Pfeffer, Gusenbauer, Weippl; N=29, grounded theory) was the first qualitative study of crypto users' mental models. The harmful misconceptions cluster around (i) cryptographic keys, (ii) anonymity and (iii) fees. Some users assumed they were anonymous or that the blockchain was encrypted. Participants held varied, wrong models of fees (set by an administrator, fixed, or chosen by miners), and a few thought blockchain transactions get deleted after some time. The authors side with Wash et al.: shape the technology "to work well with existing mental models" instead of trying to force correct ones. The study also names a "bank bias" in non-users, meaning users carry bank mental models into crypto. — [USENIX SOUPS 2020 paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/soups2020-mai.pdf); [USENIX presentation page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2020/presentation/mai)
- CHI 2025, "Of Secrets and Seedphrases" (20 interviews plus a survey of 643): per a search summary, only 43.4% of crypto users correctly identified a seed phrase, 58% believed they could choose or reset it like a password, and 52% thought a username and password was enough to recover a wallet. In other words, the "password" and "account" mental models carry over wrongly. — [ACM DL, CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713209) (percentages taken from the search-result summary; I could not read the full text)
- "What I Sign Is Not What I See" (Qin & Duan, arXiv, 23 Jan 2026; between-subjects study, N=128): users often misread key parameters in wallet signing requests, underestimated the risk of high-risk signatures, and relied on "superficial familiarity rather than understanding transaction intent". A prototype that decodes signature intent into plain-language, labeled summaries improved accuracy at spotting risky signatures and decision confidence, and lowered cognitive workload. — [arXiv 2601.16751](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16751)
- Industry sources on "approval phishing" say most wallet-drainer losses came from victims signing transactions they did not understand, not from stolen passwords or protocol hacks. Approvals can be unlimited, can last indefinitely, and look routine, which is what makes the attack work. (These are vendor and industry sources, not peer-reviewed.) — [Trust Wallet blog](https://trustwallet.com/blog/security/token-approvals-and-wallet-drainers-how-to-keep-your-assets-safe); [Group-IB](https://www.group-ib.com/resources/knowledge-hub/crypto-wallet-drainers/); [CryptoTicker](https://cryptoticker.io/en/wallet-drainer-token-approval-signature/)
- CCS 2023 found that mental models affect adoption of multi-device crypto wallets. — [ACM DL, CCS 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3576915.3623218) (abstract only)

### Inferences
- **"Certificate"** brings a strong existing model: a gift certificate, meaning prepaid money for one shop. That fits the product well (one place, prepaid, spendable by the holder). The risk is what users bring along with it: gift certificates are expected to last a long time and in the US are regulated (see §6). "Certificate" can also suggest "certificate of deposit" (a bank product) or an SSL or security certificate to developers. The copy deck's gloss "money for one place" is the right fix. Put the gloss on first use on every people-facing page, not just once per site.
- **"Seal" or "sealed" as a verb for signing**: the copy deck is right to drop it. The 2026 signing study shows users already treat "sign" as a routine click. A decorative metaphor on top ("seal") hides intent even further. What helps is showing *what* the signature authorizes ("total so far: 0.37 to Lantern Café"), which is the running-total slip design. The slip is a good, transparent metaphor because what it shows *is* the authorization.
- **"Approve"** (ERC-20 approve) is the riskiest word in the funding flow, per the approval-phishing evidence. Wherever the giver or owner sees an approval step, say in plain words exactly what is approved: the amount, and "only this contract can move up to X". Never show a bare "Approve". Recent commit 19ce948 ("an approve that went through is never asked for again") suggests this flow already exists.
- **"Wallet"**: the tagline "Hand over a budget, not your wallet" uses the everyday meaning of wallet (your money in general). Users bring the physical-wallet model to crypto wallets, and the CHI 2025 data shows that model carries over badly (password, recoverable). Since the holder phone flow deliberately says "no crypto wallet", keep "wallet" in marketing only, in its everyday sense. Consider not naming the phone route `/wallet` in visible UI. Label it with something like "My certificates" instead.
- **"Anonymous"**: SOUPS 2020 shows users *already* wrongly assume anonymity. The deck's "Not linked to names" plus "payments are public on the blockchain" goes in the right direction and should appear wherever payments are shown, not only in small print.
- **"Fees"**: users hold wrong fee models, so "never pay a fee" (holder) and "network fee" (collector) need a precise statement of who pays. That matches the deck's rule (b), which is an actual contract property.
- **"Tally", "Counting House", "till", "door", "hand over"**: these are brand metaphors with little risk of carrying over a harmful model. "Till" is British and Commonwealth English (US: "register"; Philippines: "cashier" or "counter"). "Counting House" is opaque to newcomers, which is why the deck's "Dashboard" nav label is right. "Door" (the hero toggle) is internal vocabulary and should not appear in UI copy.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study specifically on comprehension of the word "gas" or of "approve" prompts beyond industry sources and the 2026 arXiv preprint. arXiv 2407.11440 ("End-user Comprehension of Transfer Risks in Smart Contracts") looks relevant but I did not read it.
- I found no study testing "certificate" versus "budget" versus "voucher" versus "card" as labels for prepaid, merchant-locked value. This needs primary testing (see §3).

## 2. UX writing for money products: plain language, microcopy for moving money, errors, confirmations, reading level

### Takeaway
The main public-sector and fintech guides agree: write for a reading age of about 9 even for experts, one idea per sentence, sentences under about 20 words, active voice so it is clear who does what, no unexplained financial jargon, and errors that don't blame the user and give a way forward. For money movement, name the state accurately ("arrived" versus "sent" versus "initiated") and show the sequence of what has happened, what is happening, and what comes next.

### Cited Findings
- GOV.UK/GDS: write for a maximum reading age of 9, even for specialists. That means quick to scan and easy to understand, not childish. By age 9 people know about 5,000 common words by shape, and adults still recognize those faster. About 1 in 7 adults in England have literacy at or below Entry Level 3 (the skills expected of a 9–11-year-old). Keep sentences under 20 words and one idea per sentence. — [GDS blog, Writing content for everyone (2016)](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2016/02/23/writing-content-for-everyone/); [Home Office UCD manual, Readability](https://design.homeoffice.gov.uk/accessibility/written-content/readability); [ONS style guide](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/content/writing-for-users/plain-language)
- NN/g: plain language is for everyone, even experts. Highly educated domain experts also wanted succinct, scannable text, and one IT manager rejected an article for branded terms he didn't understand ("a whole lot of jargon and technical terms I'm not aware of"). Plain language also makes the writer look more competent. — [NN/g, Plain Language Is for Everyone, Even Experts](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/)
- Monzo's tone of voice: "no room at all for unexplained financial jargon". Beware the curse of knowledge. Prefer everyday words ("need, give" over "require, provide"; "We're sorry" over "We'd like to apologise"). Use active voice so responsibility is clear. For bad news, lead with the impact, and don't apologize when you're not at fault (it reads as insincere). Read copy aloud. Avoid idioms for non-native speakers. — [Monzo tone of voice](https://monzo.com/tone-of-voice)
- Mailchimp content style guide: no exclamation points in failure messages or alerts. Adjust tone to the reader's emotional state (calm for errors, warmer for success). Errors shouldn't blame the user, should be specific rather than technical, and should offer a next step. — [Mailchimp Voice and Tone](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/); [Mailchimp Grammar and Mechanics](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/grammar-and-mechanics/)
- Wise's transparency pattern: make a transfer visible as it moves, showing what has already happened, what is happening now, and what should happen next, so users follow a sequence instead of trusting blindly. In fintech microcopy, "Arrived" confirms finality better than "Sent", and "Initiated" fits money that hasn't cleared yet. (Secondary analyses, not Wise's own style guide.) — [Raw.Studio on Wise](https://raw.studio/blog/how-wise-uses-3-transparency-first-ux-principles/); [UX Writing Hub, Fintech UX writing](https://uxwritinghub.com/fintech-ux-writing-and-content-design/)
- Stripe keeps a public glossary of the terms it uses (for example "3D Secure"). This is a pattern for developer docs: define each branded or technical term once, in one place, and link to it. — [Stripe Glossary](https://docs.stripe.com/glossary)
- A US SEC paper (Jan 2026) looks at jargon in consumer information on mutual fund fees, which shows regulators are paying attention to jargon comprehension in fee disclosures. — [SEC, Jargon in Consumer Information: The Case of Mutual Fund Fees](https://www.sec.gov/files/jargon-mutual-fund-fees.pdf) (not read in full)

### Inferences (applied to the copy deck)
- The copy deck's till statuses mostly follow these rules: a status word first ("Accepted", "Rejected"), then a specific reason, then an action. Suggestions:
  - "Rejected" sounds like a verdict on the customer. At the counter, Mailchimp-style guidance favors something neutral and specific: "Not accepted: this certificate has ended". The problem-plus-next-step pattern is already present in "Ask for a new one".
  - "This code wasn't signed by the certificate's holder" asks the cashier to understand signing. A plainer version: "Not accepted: this code didn't come from this certificate's phone. Ask the customer to try again."
  - Keep "Unverified · merchant risk" but lead with impact (Monzo): "You could lose up to 5.00 if this payment is bad."
- Use state words that match what is true on-chain. The payment lifecycle should read like Wise's timeline: Signed, then Accepted by you (off-chain), then Collected (on-chain), then Leftovers returned. Never show "Paid" or "Received" before collection if the money is still in the contract. "Accepted" plus the claim-(a) backing is accurate. "Received" would overstate it.
- Confirmation screens should repeat the whole action in one sentence: who, how much, where, until when, and what happens to leftovers. The deck's "Give Mia 20.00 USDC at Lantern Café for 30 days. Unused money returns to you on 24 Oct." is already a model example and passes the reading-age test.
- Agent error messages written for LLMs ("Don't retry the payment") should follow the same plain rules. Agents also benefit from a stable machine code (`insufficient`) next to the prose, a pattern the deck already uses.
- Reading-level target: aim for grade 5–6 (reading age of about 9–11) on people-facing surfaces (/shops, wallet, till), and grade 8–9 on developer docs. Check with any Flesch-Kincaid tool before shipping. The GDS rule applies to specialists too.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch Wise's own public writing guidelines, if they exist. The Wise findings come from secondary analyses.
- I found no fintech-specific quantitative study linking microcopy wording to conversion or trust in 2024–2026.

## 3. Naming: jargon versus familiar words, how to test terminology, and whether terms should differ by audience (SDK "certificate" versus consumer "budget")

### Takeaway
Use the users' own words on user-facing surfaces and test them with methods that avoid keyword matching (open card sorts, neutral labels, tree tests, first-click tests). Keeping one internal or code term while showing different labels to different audiences is common and fine, *as long as* each surface is consistent and a public glossary maps the terms to each other. The copy deck's "never both vocabularies on one screen" rule matches that principle.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g: in card sorting, labels must be neutral to avoid keyword matching (participants grouping by shared words rather than meaning). Avoid card text that repeats category names. — [NN/g, Card Sorting: Pushing Users Beyond Terminology Matches](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-terminology-matches/)
- NN/g: card sorting finds users' mental models and grouping ideas, while tree testing checks whether users can find items with your labels and hierarchy. Use them together: card sort to generate, tree test to validate. — [NN/g, Card Sorting vs. Tree Testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-tree-testing-differences/); [NN/g, Tree Testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tree-testing/); [NN/g, Card Sorting definition](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-definition/)
- NN/g experts study: branded or unfamiliar terms hurt comprehension even for experts (see §2). — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/)
- SOUPS 2020 notes a "cryptocurrency tool bias": the terms and interfaces of existing tools shape users' mental models. Naming in the tool *teaches* the model. — [USENIX SOUPS 2020](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/soups2020-mai.pdf)
- Stripe's pattern: one canonical glossary for the terms used across docs and product. — [Stripe Glossary](https://docs.stripe.com/glossary)

### Inferences
- **Split by audience.** The code (`Certificate`, `Note`, `redeem`, `funder`, `spender`, `payee`) is read by developers and by AI agents reading docs and `llms.txt`. Consumer UI is read by givers, holders and cashiers. Recommended mapping:
  - SDK, ABI and contract: keep the code names (renaming breaks the sealed spec).
  - Developer docs: introduce each code name once with its plain gloss, e.g. "`Certificate` (a budget: USDC set aside for one seller, one spender, until an end date)". Use "budget" in prose and `Certificate` in code.
  - Agent-facing UI and errors: use "budget".
  - People-facing UI: use "certificate" with the "money for one place" gloss.

  A single `/docs/glossary` page with a three-column table (Code term · Agent surface · People surface) keeps LLM agents and humans aligned. AI agents especially need the synonym mapping stated explicitly, or they will treat "budget" and "certificate" as different objects.
- **"Redeem" to "Collect"**: good. "Redeem" in consumer finance means the *holder* cashing in (points, vouchers), but here the *seller* collects, so "redeem" would carry over the wrong actor. Keep `redeem` only in code.
- **"Places" and "People"** as dashboard tabs are clear. "Holders" is less natural than "People" for a consumer ("holder" is securities jargon). Test "People" against "Holders" in a tree test.
- **Suggested testing plan** (cheap, fits a solo founder):
  1. An open card sort with about 15 participants per audience (parents or givers, café owners, developers) using neutral task descriptions such as "money you set aside for your kid at the canteen". Ask them to name the groups, and harvest their words (budget / allowance / voucher / tab / credit / certificate / card).
  2. A tree test of the Counting House tabs and nav (Give / Holders / Places / Requests / Collect).
  3. A first-click test on the till's status screens.
  4. A comprehension check ("What happens to money Mia doesn't spend?", "Can you cancel this?") after reading the certificate card. That checks the *right model*, not just findability.

  I found no published benchmark sample sizes in this search. NN/g's widely cited ~15-participant guidance for card sorting comes from its general articles and was not re-verified here.

### Gaps
- I found no published research on keeping different terminologies for developer and consumer audiences in the same product. The recommendation is inferred from general consistency principles and Stripe's glossary practice.
- I found no research on how LLM agents interpret synonymous terms in docs. It is plausible that explicit glossary mapping helps, but that is unverified.

## 4. Trust signals in visual design: aesthetics, credibility, color and type, distinctive versus generic "AI slop", and a Chinese historical motif used globally

### Takeaway
People judge credibility first from the visual "design look". Attractive interfaces are perceived as easier to use and forgive minor problems, but not broken ones. In 2025–2026 the generic AI-generated look (Inter, indigo-to-purple gradients, three rounded cards) has become a recognized sign of low effort, so a distinctive, crafted identity is a trust asset in itself. A Chinese historical motif can be that differentiator if it is used with context and accuracy rather than as decoration. The main risk is using characters and imagery as flattened "exotic" surface.

### Cited Findings
- Stanford Web Credibility Project (Fogg et al.; more than 3 years, over 5,500 participants): "design look" was the most prominent factor people cited when judging a site's credibility, mentioned in nearly half of comments. Guideline: "Design your site so it looks professional (or is appropriate for your purpose)", paying attention to layout, typography, images and consistency. Other Stanford guidelines include making it easy to verify claims, showing there is a real organization behind the site, and avoiding errors of all types. — [Stanford Web Credibility Guidelines](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html); [Prominence-Interpretation Theory (PDF)](https://credibility.stanford.edu/pdf/PITheory.pdf); [Encyclopedia MDPI summary](https://encyclopedia.pub/entry/33707)
- NN/g, aesthetic-usability effect: users see attractive products as more usable and tolerate *minor* issues. The effect does not cover crashes, slow loads or broken links, it fades with repeated use, and it can hide problems during usability testing. — [NN/g, Aesthetic-Usability Effect](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/)
- The "AI slop" look in 2026 is widely described as the Inter typeface, an indigo-to-purple (Tailwind blue-600 to purple-500/pink-500) gradient, three rounded cards in a row, and a generic hero. Commentators explain it as LLMs returning the statistical median of their training data (Tailwind defaults since about 2019), and people recognize it as machine-made in about half a second. Tools such as "Hallmark" (an MIT-licensed design skill) exist to steer agents away from it. (These are practitioner blogs, not research.) — [DEV: The Purple Gradient Problem](https://dev.to/james_anderson_h/the-purple-gradient-problem-why-ai-ui-all-looks-alike-and-how-to-fix-it-3j65); [925studios: AI slop design tells](https://www.925studios.co/blog/ai-slop-design-tells); [SmoothUI: AI design slop](https://smoothui.dev/blog/ai-design-slop); [Medium, Aug 2026](https://mohitphogat.medium.com/ai-design-slop-why-every-ai-built-interface-looks-the-same-and-how-to-fix-it-bf874e0b470c)
- On Chinese imagery in Western branding: scholar Lei Ping and others criticize using Chinese characters and motifs as a "flattened, smooth and harmless Other", surface decoration that asks no cultural understanding of the audience. — [US-China Today, Lei Ping interview (2019)](https://uschinatoday.org/qa/2019/12/19/when-cultural-appreciation-becomes-appropriation-lei-ping-on-chinese-imagery-in-western-fashion/); [Jing Daily: Chinese characters in fashion](https://jingdaily.com/posts/chinese-characters-fashion-design); [elsewhere-zine](https://elsewhere-zine.com/en/discussing-fashion-orientalism-and-cultural-appropriation-en/)
- On bilingual Chinese and Latin logo systems: the recommended approach is to match stroke logic, curves, weight and color across both scripts so each version keeps the same identity. — [Heirloom on Medium: Two names, one logo](https://medium.com/@Heirloom/two-names-one-logo-integrating-chinese-and-non-chinese-names-in-logo-design-51d3510cd254)
- The FDIC's 2024 Part 328 rules and enforcement (see §6) show that visual and verbal cues suggesting bank or deposit-insurance status are a regulatory concern, not just a design one. — [eCFR 12 CFR 328 Subpart B](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-328/subpart-B)

### Inferences
- **Credibility checklist mapped to Stanford guidelines**:
  - "Easy to verify": link to the verified contract on the explorer, the test suites and the audit status. The deck's status line already does this.
  - "A real organization behind it": add a named maintainer and a contact on /about.
  - "Avoid errors": broken demo states hurt trust more than any aesthetic gain can make up for, per the limits of the aesthetic-usability effect.
  - Honest risk disclosure (the "Not protected" list) is itself a credibility signal, in line with Wise's transparency pattern.
- **A distinctive identity rather than generic fintech**: the seal mark, tally art and certificate cards give Flying Money a real way to avoid the AI-slop look. Guidance:
  - Build the palette from the source material (for example cinnabar or vermilion seal red, paper and ink neutrals) instead of purple gradients.
  - Use a type pairing that is not Inter-only. A serif or engraved-style face for amounts on certificates echoes banknote and document typography.
  - Keep functional UI (buttons, forms, status) conventional and high-contrast. Put the metaphor in the *artifacts* (certificate card, seal, tally), not in controls. This follows the NN/g skeuomorphism lesson: realism in controls hurts, while recognizable objects help learning.
- **Red means two different things**. In Western finance UIs red means error or loss. In Chinese contexts red means luck, and a vermilion seal means authenticity. If the seal red is also the brand color, keep a separate hue for "Rejected" and error states (or pair every error with an icon and text) so a seal never reads as an error and vice versa.
- **The seal as a trust signal**: in Chinese documents a seal (印章) means authorization. The till shows "Accepted" when a slip is valid, so a seal visual works best as the *Accepted* or *signed* mark on slips and certificates, where it carries a true meaning. Using it decoratively weakens it.
- **Chinese characters for non-Chinese readers**: 飛錢 (traditional script; simplified 飞钱) will read as ornament to most global users. Treat it as a mark with a romanized and translated caption ("fēi qián, 'flying money'") on first appearance. Don't use characters as fake text or pattern filler. Get historical claims checked (the copy deck's "804" and the matching-halves "tally" description) by a sinologist or at least a primary source. Inaccurate history is the fastest way for a heritage motif to read as appropriation. Consider crediting sources on /story. Choose traditional script, the historically accurate choice for Tang-era texts, and use it consistently.
- **Test the motif** with Chinese-heritage users in target markets (Philippines has a large Chinese-Filipino community; also Singapore and Malaysia) for tone. This is a gap, not a finding.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous 2020–2026 studies on color and typography effects on trust specifically in fintech. Claims such as "blue signals trust" are widespread but I found no primary evidence in this search, so I did not include them.
- I found no empirical study on how non-Chinese audiences perceive Chinese characters in fintech or tech branding. The appropriation sources are commentary on fashion.
- I did not verify the historical claims about Tang flying money (date 804, matching halves). Another research stream should check them.

## 5. Localization and internationalization for money UIs (Philippines, LatAm, RTL)

### Takeaway
Never format amounts by hand. Use locale-aware formatting (`Intl.NumberFormat`) with the locale passed explicitly. The Philippines uses Anglo-style separators (₱1,234.56), while Brazil (pt-BR) and much of LatAm use a decimal comma (1.234,56), which can misread amounts by a factor of 1,000 if handled wrong. Keep numbers left-to-right even in RTL layouts, and use CSS logical properties.

### Cited Findings
- Philippines (en_PH): ₱ (U+20B1) before the number, comma for thousands, period for decimals, 2 decimals, e.g. ₱1,234.56. — [Freeformatter, Philippines standards](https://www.freeformatter.com/philippines-standards-code-snippets.html); [Wikipedia, Philippine peso sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_peso_sign)
- pt-BR, de-DE and fr-FR use a comma as the decimal separator, en-US a period. `Intl.NumberFormat` handles separators, symbols and placement, and the locale should be passed explicitly (for example to avoid SSR hydration mismatches). `minimumFractionDigits` and `maximumFractionDigits` control decimals. — [Front-End Checklist: Intl currency formatting](https://frontendchecklist.io/rules/i18n/currency-formatting); [W3C i18n: Number, currency and unit formatting](https://w3c.github.io/i18n-drafts/questions/qa-number-format.en.html); [Phrase: number localization](https://phrase.com/blog/posts/number-localization/)
- Symbol position varies (prefix in USD $100, suffix in SEK 100 kr). In RTL scripts (Arabic, Hebrew, Persian) numbers are still written LTR, and layouts should use logical properties (margin-inline-start and similar) rather than left and right. — [Front-End Checklist](https://frontendchecklist.io/rules/i18n/currency-formatting); [DEV: localization for frontend devs](https://dev.to/kiransm/frontend-developers-2oc3); [UX Patterns: currency input](https://uxpatterns.dev/patterns/forms/currency-input)
- The SOUPS 2020 advice to avoid idioms for non-native speakers is echoed by Monzo. — [Monzo tone of voice](https://monzo.com/tone-of-voice)

### Inferences
- **USDC is not a fiat currency code** in CLDR, so `Intl.NumberFormat` with `style: 'currency'` won't know it. Format the number with the locale (decimal style, 2 fraction digits for display) and add "USDC" as a unit label. Place it consistently after the number ("20.00 USDC"), as the deck already does. Amounts are `bigint` base units (6 decimals for USDC). Convert to a display string with integer math, then localize the separators. Never use floats (this matches AGENTS.md).
- **Input fields in comma-decimal locales**: a Brazilian user typing "3,50" must not become 350. Parse input by locale, or use a digits-only "cents-shift" input. Always echo the parsed amount back in words or with the unit on the review screen ("Pay 3.50 USDC"). Test this before any LatAm pilot.
- **Philippines**: English is widely used in business UI, so the en-PH format works. Copy may later need Filipino (Tagalog) localization. The words "till" and "canteen" are understood in the Philippines ("canteen" is common in Philippine schools, which supports the Door B example), but check them with local users.
- **Showing a local-currency equivalent** ("≈ ₱1,120") helps comprehension but brings in a rate source and a risk of implied promises. Label it as approximate and give the time and source, or leave it out.
- **Dates**: "ends 24 Oct" is ambiguous across locales when shown numerically (10/24 versus 24/10). Always use a month name, and format with `Intl.DateTimeFormat` using the user's locale.
- **Translation expansion**: Spanish and Portuguese strings often run longer than English. Till status buttons need room.

### Gaps
- I found no source on terminology preferences for prepaid or merchant-locked value in Filipino, Spanish or Portuguese (for example "load" is the common Philippine word for prepaid top-up, which is anecdotal and unverified here).
- I found no Wise-authored public localization guidance.

## 6. Wording constraints: avoiding terms that imply regulated products (bank, account, insured, safe, gift certificate)

### Takeaway
In the US, representing a non-deposit or crypto product as FDIC-insured, or blurring it with deposit products, is explicitly in scope of FDIC Part 328 (amended 2024, fully in effect 1 Jan 2025), and the FDIC has ordered crypto firms to stop such claims. Separately, the words "gift certificate" and "gift card" carry specific US rules under Regulation E §1005.20 (CARD Act), including a minimum 5-year expiration for funds. That is directly relevant to a "certificate" with a 30-day end date sold as "a gift for one shop". Copy should avoid bank, deposit, account, insured, interest and "safe" language, and qualify "certificate" and "gift".

### Cited Findings
- FDIC Part 328 Subpart B covers false advertising, misrepresentation of insured status and misuse of the FDIC name or logo. The 2023–2024 rulemaking made clear that representations about crypto-assets are in scope, and it defines "non-deposit products" to include crypto-assets. It was effective 1 Apr 2024, with full compliance by 1 Jan 2025. — [eCFR 12 CFR 328 Subpart B](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-328/subpart-B); [Federal Register final rule, 18 Jan 2024](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/01/18/2023-28629/fdic-official-signs-and-advertising-requirements-false-advertising-misrepresentation-of-insured); [Consumer Finance Monitor](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2024/03/28/fdic-final-signage-and-false-advertising-rule-will-be-effective-april-1-2024-with-an-extended-full-compliance-date-of-january-1-2025/)
- The FDIC's concern is that offering insured deposits alongside non-deposit products without clear disclosure may lead consumers to assume everything is insured. The rule requires clear separation, and disclosure when a fintech is not itself an insured bank. In 2022 the FDIC ordered five crypto companies to stop false FDIC-insurance claims. — [FDIC Q&A (2024)](https://www.fdic.gov/news/financial-institution-letters/2024/questions-and-answers-regarding-fdic-official-signs-and); [Luthor summary, 2026](https://www.luthor.ai/resources/fdic-advertising) (secondary)
- Regulation E §1005.20 (CARD Act): a gift certificate, store gift card or general-use prepaid card may not be sold or issued with funds that expire less than 5 years after issuance or last load, subject to defined exclusions. The rule also restricts dormancy and inactivity fees and requires disclosures. — [CFPB, §1005.20](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/20/); [Cornell LII, 12 CFR 1005.20](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1005.20); [CFPB official interpretation](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/Interp-20); [Nolo overview](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/question-legal-gift-certificates-expire-27997.html)
- SOUPS 2020 found "bank bias": non-users map crypto onto bank mental models. That is exactly the carry-over that terms like "account" or "deposit" would strengthen. — [USENIX SOUPS 2020](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/soups2020-mai.pdf)

### Inferences (not legal advice; for counsel review)
- The copy deck already avoids "bank", "insured", "escrow", "guaranteed" and "safe", and uses "set aside" and "backed by money set aside for [seller]". Keep that. Remaining words to audit:
  - "to your account" in the till's Collect hint ("move the day's payments to your account"). Prefer "to your address" or "to your USDC address".
  - "Mode banner… Real USDC". Fine, but never pair it with any "protected" or "insured" wording.
  - The Counting House page title. "House" plus "Counting" might suggest a financial institution. It is low risk because it is historical and branded, but it should never appear with "deposit".
- **"Certificate" plus "gift" plus "end date" is the highest-regulatory-sensitivity combination in the vocabulary.** The use-case card "A gift for one shop… Unspent money returns to the sender after the end date" describes something a US consumer, or a regulator, could read as a gift certificate with a short expiry. Mitigations to discuss with counsel:
  1. Avoid "gift certificate" or "gift card" phrasing in US-facing copy.
  2. Frame it as the *giver's own money set aside for a period and returned*. Nothing is forfeited, which differs from the CARD Act's harm (expired funds lost to the issuer). Say "Returns to you" prominently.
  3. Keep the test-network status explicit.

  Whether §1005.20 or its exclusions apply to a non-custodial smart-contract arrangement is a legal question. I found no source on that.
- **"Certificate of deposit" collision**: to a US consumer, "certificate" plus "money set aside" plus "until an end date" closely resembles the structure of a CD (an insured bank deposit). Given Part 328's crypto scope, never place "certificate" near "deposit", "interest", "earn" or "term". This supports using "budget" on agent surfaces and keeping the "money for one place" gloss for people.
- **"Safe" and "protected"**: the deck's "What's protected, and what isn't" is a strong honest pattern. "Protected" is less loaded than "insured" or "safe", but it should always be scoped ("the spender can't pay more than the budget"), never stand alone.
- **Other jurisdictions** (Philippines: BSP e-money and VASP rules; Brazil: the BCB crypto framework; UK: FCA financial promotions for cryptoassets) likely constrain wording too. I did not research them here (gap).
